# Published frameworks the method leans on

Dated 2026-10-15. Each work is cited by title and author, as a bibliography would, and summarised in the
skill's own words; the summaries are paraphrased; short framework names and formulations are retained. The course's own facilitator wrote two of the supporting articles;
by this skill's rule the course's people and their firms are not named, so those two are cited by title
alone. Where an attribution comes from the builder's general knowledge rather than the material, it says so.

| Framework | Work | What the method takes from it | Where the skill uses it | Rows |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The big ideaL | "The Red Papers: What's The big ideaL?", Ogilvy & Mather (2010) | A brand's organising belief sits where a cultural tension meets the brand's best self, and is written as a short structured sentence about how the world would be better. A tension is not a trend. The best self is not a list of benefits; ask loyal users, look at heritage. The workshop runs in three chunks (tension, best self, formulation), does not wordsmith in the room, and a small team settles the wording after. A finished line is judged by whether the brand can live up to it, whether it will get noticed and whether it excites the people who made it. It does not replace a definition of voice, look and feel. | Steps 3 to 8; the purpose second check; the scope line on visual identity | W-148, W-149, W-150, W-151, W-152, W-153, W-154 |
| Hierarchy of needs | "A Theory of Human Motivation", A. H. Maslow (1943) | A ladder of needs from the physiological to self-fulfilment. In the course it is shown as a prompt before the benefits pyramid, not reproduced as an exercise. | Step 16, as a prompt when listing benefits | W-055 |
| The elements of value | "The Elements of Value", Almquist, Senior and Bloch, Harvard Business Review (2016); the attribution is from the builder's general knowledge, the course deck shows the pyramid without a citation | About thirty elements in four bands from functional through emotional and life-changing to social impact. The course's use: find the company's elements, then lead with the functional ones in early messaging. Cited, not reproduced. | Step 16, as a prompt | W-206, W-207 |
| Functional-to-emotional spectrum | The course's own exercise, drawn as a line from functional to emotional, and its facilitator's article "Great Startups Deserve Great Brands" | List functional, emotional and in-between benefits; an early-stage company stays near the functional end because it has not yet earned allusive messaging. | Step 16 | W-056, W-128, W-057 |
| Language/market fit | "Finding Language/Market Fit: How to Make Customers Feel Like You've Read Their Minds", Matt Lerner | Prospects scan for words that match the goals and struggles already in their heads. Four steps: interview recent buyers about their goals, struggles and the words they use; draft headlines that complete "Now you can ..." rather than "Our product is ..."; validate comprehension with a five-second test (show the line, take it away, ask what it said and what it meant; four people per line); then test quantitatively. Avoid platitudes; stay specific. | Steps 4, 15, 16 and 21; the validation kit | W-144, W-145, W-146, W-147, W-211 |
| Positioning | "Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind", Al Ries and Jack Trout (McGraw-Hill, 2001 edition) | Positioning happens in the prospect's mind, not in the product. The easy way in is to be first; if you cannot be first in a category, set up a new category you can be first in. A follower looks for the hole in the mind. The everybody trap: a brand that tries to appeal to everyone stands for nothing, so ask who should not use it. The six questions for a positioning programme: what position you own, what you want to own, whom you must outgun, whether you have the money, whether you can stick it out, whether you match your position. | Steps 10, 13, 14 and 19; the "commits the company to" line on every candidate | W-157, W-158, W-156 |
| Category choice | The course and its facilitator's article "Great Startups Deserve Great Brands" | Three choices: play in an existing category (only if the company could someday win it), modify an existing one (when the market is too big to win outright), create a new one (slow and expensive; only if the company can claim first, only or best and afford to repeat the name for years). In B2B, buyers hold budget for existing categories, so existing or modified is often the better move. | Step 14 | W-042, W-126, W-049, W-045 |
| Dimensions of brand personality | "Dimensions of Brand Personality", Jennifer L. Aaker, Journal of Marketing Research (1997) | Brand personality resolves into five dimensions: sincerity, excitement, competence, sophistication, ruggedness. The course's reading: a strong brand spikes in two. | Step 23 | W-087, W-131 |
| The golden circle | "How Great Leaders Inspire Action", Simon Sinek, TED talk (2009) | Why, how, what: everyone knows what they do, some know how, few know why; the inspiring ones communicate from the inside out, starting with why. | Step 22: the three-line summary runs why, what, how | W-155 |
| Minimum viable brand strategy | The course, and its facilitator's article "Great Startups Deserve Great Brands" | Purpose, then positioning, then personality, in that order; purpose on a ten-year horizon, positioning on about eighteen months. Purpose must settle decisions or it is not a purpose. The litmus: three months on, everyone at the company gives the same answer to why it exists. The foil is usually the old way, not another start-up. Jargon out; explain it so a bored but smart teenager gets it; poke the problem before the features. Brainstorm many adjectives and keep the five hardest-working and most different; the top of the star is the one to own. The best style guides cover how the brand writes, not only how it looks. | Steps 2, 7, 14, 16, 17, 24, 26, 29 and 31 | W-120, W-121, W-122, W-123, W-124, W-125, W-127, W-129, W-130, W-132, W-133 |
| The positioning statement's template and its uses | The course, and its facilitator's article "Positioning Your Startup is Vital, Here's How to Nail It" | The six-line statement is internal, not a tagline; one dominant statement everything rolls up to. Pre-work: what is different about how it works, why you do it, the broadest circle of customers narrowed to one model user, the pain points and their emotions, who else solves it and how. If a decent statement cannot be written, that is a sign of a product problem, not a wording one. | Steps 1, 15, 19 | W-134, W-135, W-136 |
