# Defined terms

Dated 2026-10-15. The method's vocabulary in the skill's own words, with the ledger rows each rests on.
Where the course leaves a term undefined, the owner's shape (2026-10-15) is marked as such.

| Term | Meaning | Rows |
|---|---|---|
| Brand | Who people think the company is; far more than a logo. Three parts: purpose, position, personality. | W-110, W-114 |
| Purpose | The change the company wants to see in the world, irrespective of financial gain; its "why". One statement, in place of separate mission and vision statements. On a ten-year horizon. | W-255, W-174, W-123, W-003, W-121 |
| Position | What makes the company unique and different, relative to what its audience already knows. On a horizon of about eighteen months. | W-256, W-121 |
| Personality | How the company shows up in the world; if it were a person, what it would be like. | W-257 |
| Cultural tension | Something happening in the world that matters to the company's customers, including assumptions they hold without noticing. Not a trend: a tension arises where trends collide, or where a trend provokes a counter-reaction. | W-177, W-150 |
| Brand's best self | What the company delivers when it is at its best, as a top-line summary, not a list of features or benefits. Found in customers' words, especially loyal ones, and in the company's history; it may not be what the company is today. | W-178, W-014, W-151 |
| The big ideal | The point where a cultural tension meets the brand's best self; the company's point of view and organising principle. More emotional than the positioning statement, which rests on a functional benefit; supported by the functional. | W-010, W-179, W-149 |
| Purpose sentence | Two forms: "For [company], the world would be a better place if ..." and "We exist to ...". | W-011, W-020 |
| Marks of a good purpose | Inspiring (people want to root for it); clear (no jargon); actionable (it settles company decisions); on a ten-year horizon (room to change); bonus if it feels like a quest. | W-007, W-170, W-171, W-172, W-173 |
| Concentric circles of audience | Four nested circles, each a subset of the one before, from circle 1 to circle 4, then a model persona inside circle 4. | W-033, W-036 |
| Circle 1 | The total addressable market for all time. | W-191 |
| Circle 4 | The target audience for the next eighteen months. The "For" line of the positioning statement. | W-192, W-050 |
| Observable | A circle is described by something a stranger could see or verify (a behaviour, a situation, a count), never by a state of mind alone and never by wanting the product. | W-037, W-035 |
| Model persona | One person who stands for circle 4, as a short paragraph: a first name, age or stage, role, the situation that puts them in circle 4, what they do about the problem today, one or two telling habits. A picture of circle 4, not a second audience; an unknown field stays TBD. (Owner's shape; the course shows examples and gives no definition.) | W-193, W-198 |
| Category | The mental model the audience uses for the company: the Y in "X is a Y that does Z". Three choices: an existing category, a modified existing one, a new one. | W-125, W-042 |
| First, only or best | The question a category must answer: can the company be the first, the only or the best in it? | W-045, W-157 |
| Positioning statement | Six lines, internal, not a tagline: For (circle 4) / Who (the customer's situation or struggle, never a wish for the product) / is a (category) / That (key benefit) / Unlike (foil) / (differentiator). One dominant statement everything rolls up to. | W-030, W-050, W-051, W-134 |
| Key benefit | What one happy user tells another about the product, in words people actually say. Chosen from a spectrum that runs from functional to emotional; a young company leads with the functional end. | W-058, W-056, W-057, W-061 |
| Primary competitor, the foil | The competing alternative the company is up against most. Often not a company: the old way of doing things, the status quo, or doing nothing. | W-063, W-065, W-127 |
| Differentiator | How the product does what the benefit promises: a reason to believe the benefit, something the foil cannot claim, and not a list of more benefits. | W-216, W-069, W-217, W-218 |
| Bar test | Two people in circle 4 catching up: "I just started using [product]" / "What is it?" / "It's this new [category] that [benefit]" / "Tell me more" / "Well, instead of [foil], [differentiator]". Then three questions: does the benefit make you want to learn more; does the differentiator explain the benefit; do people talk like this. If it cannot be said aloud, it is not done. | W-072, W-073, W-220, W-221, W-129 |
| Swap test | The chosen statement beside the foil's current copy (where the foil is a company) and each named competitor's copy supplied: could any of them sign it with the names changed? If so, the positioning is not distinctive. (Owner's check, 2026-10-15, anchored to the course's done-mark "distinctive".) | W-251, W-063 |
| Three-line summary | We exist to ... (purpose, why) / We do this through ... (category and benefit, what) / This is made possible by ... (differentiator, how). The connectives "through" and "by" are the owner's ruling where the course's slides differed. | W-077, W-078 |
| Five dimensions of brand personality | Sincerity (down to earth, honest); excitement (spirited, up to date); competence (reliable, intelligent); sophistication (charming, upper class); ruggedness (outdoorsy, tough). A strong brand spikes in two. | W-087, W-226, W-227, W-228, W-229, W-230, W-131 |
| Tension (personality) | Adjectives that pull against each other make a brand interesting, as they do a person; five near-synonyms say one thing five times. | W-090, W-132 |
| Trait with its edge | Each of the five traits as a short sentence or two of plain prose that carries its edge: what the trait would become taken too far, not its opposite. The course's line form, "We are X, but not Y", is superseded for the document by the owner's ruling (2026-10-15) and may still be used in the working notes to find the edge. | W-260, W-094, W-095 |
| The star | The five adjectives on the five points; the top point is the one adjective the company would own; lines across the star join points in tension. | W-096, W-101, W-132 |
| "Human" | Not a personality attribute. | W-093 |
| Brand persona sentence | Optional: one sentence naming the kind of person the brand would be, which the five traits then unpack. (Owner's addition; the course shows one in an example and never asks for it.) | W-240 |
| Voice card | One per trait, four cells in the working notes: a boring sentence; not like this (jargon); not like this either (overdone); we write like this. The document's Voice section carries, per trait, what the company avoids ("Not:") and what it writes instead ("We write:"). The skill's output stops at words; visual identity is out of scope. | W-102, W-107, W-133, W-258, W-260 |
| Language/market fit | The exact words that match the goals and struggles already in prospects' heads, so that reading them feels like being understood. | W-144 |
| Validated | Said of a part only after people in circle 4 have heard it (bar test aloud, interviews, five-second test) or reactions to the purpose have come back; until then, unvalidated. | W-076, W-074, W-147, W-027 |
| The three-month litmus | Ask everyone at the company why it does what it does; three months after the document is shared, they all give the same answer. | W-122, W-164 |
